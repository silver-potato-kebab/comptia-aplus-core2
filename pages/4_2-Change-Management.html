OVERVIEWIntroduction to Change Management

Change management is the formal, structured process an organization follows before making a modification to its IT systems — a software update, a network reconfiguration, a hardware swap, a firewall rule change — so that the change happens deliberately, with known risks, a plan for what to do if it fails, and buy-in from the people it affects. Without change management, even a well-intentioned fix can cause an outage nobody saw coming, simply because nobody checked what else depended on the thing being changed.

Objective 4.2 covers two closely linked halves. Documented business processes are the standing safety nets that should exist around any change — a rollback plan, a backup plan, sandbox testing, and clearly assigned responsible staff — regardless of how the change request itself is processed. The change management workflow is the actual step-by-step process a proposed change moves through, from the initial request form to final end-user acceptance.

Core Concept — Change Management Exists to Prevent Surprises

Every element of this objective answers one of three questions: What could go wrong? (risk analysis, sandbox testing), What do we do if it does? (rollback plan, backup plan), and Who needs to know and approve before it happens? (change board approvals, responsible staff members, end-user acceptance). A technician who understands why each element exists — not just its name — can reason through any change management scenario question, even ones phrased in unfamiliar ways.

SECTION 1Documented Business Processes

These four elements form the baseline safety net that should surround any meaningful change, independent of the formal approval workflow covered in Section 2. Think of them as the preparation work that makes a change safe to attempt in the first place.

Rollback plan A documented, specific procedure for undoing the change and returning the system to its prior working state if the change fails or causes unexpected problems. A rollback plan must be prepared before the change is implemented — discovering mid-outage that there's no way back is exactly the failure this element exists to prevent.
Backup plan Ensuring a current, verified backup of the affected system or data exists immediately before the change is made, so that even if the rollback procedure itself fails, the data and configuration can still be restored from a known-good backup. This directly ties into Objective 4.3's backup and recovery methods.
Sandbox testing Testing the proposed change first in an isolated, non-production environment that mirrors production closely enough to reveal problems — without any risk to real systems or users. Sandbox testing is how many risks get caught and addressed before the change ever reaches the risk-analysis or approval stage.
Responsible staff members Clearly naming who is accountable for planning, implementing, monitoring, and — if necessary — rolling back the change. Without a named owner, a failing change can sit unresolved simply because no single person feels clearly responsible for reacting to it.

Why Skipping These Steps Is a Common Exam Trap

Scenario questions often describe a technician who made a change directly in production without testing it first, or who didn't have a rollback plan ready when something broke. The correct answer nearly always identifies the missing documented business process — not a technical detail of the change itself. Recognize that the failure described is a process failure, and match it to whichever of these four elements was skipped.

SECTION 2The Change Management Workflow

Where Section 1 covers the safety net around a change, this section covers the formal process a proposed change actually travels through — from someone first requesting it to the moment users have accepted the result.

Request Forms

A change request form is the standardized document used to formally propose a change, capturing every detail decision-makers need to evaluate it — often including the purpose, scope, change type, timing, affected systems, and risk information covered in the remaining sub-topics below. Requiring a standardized form (rather than an informal request) ensures nothing critical gets omitted and creates a written record for later audit or reference.

Purpose of the Change

A clear statement of why the change is being made — what problem it solves, what capability it adds, or what requirement it satisfies. Purpose is the first thing a change board or approver evaluates, because a change without a clear justification is difficult to weigh against its risk.

Scope of the Change

A precise definition of exactly what the change will and will not touch — which systems, configurations, or user groups are included, and just as importantly, which are explicitly excluded. A well-defined scope prevents "scope creep," where a change quietly grows to affect more than what was originally reviewed and approved.

Change Type

The exam names three distinct categories of change, each carrying a different level of scrutiny and approval speed.

Standard ChangePre-approved, low risk
Definition A routine, well-understood, low-risk change that has been performed successfully many times before, following an established and pre-approved procedure
Approval path Pre-authorized in advance — does not require case-by-case change board review each time it's performed, since the risk and procedure are already well known
Example Installing a routine, tested monthly security patch using an established, previously validated procedure
Normal ChangeFull review process
Definition A change that isn't routine or pre-approved, and doesn't need to happen immediately — it goes through the complete change management process: request, risk analysis, and change board review before implementation
Approval path Requires full change board review and approval prior to implementation; this is the default path for most non-routine changes
Example Migrating a department's file server to new hardware, planned and scheduled weeks in advance
Emergency ChangeUrgent, expedited review
Definition A change needed immediately to resolve a critical, active problem — a security incident, an outage, a severe vulnerability actively being exploited — where waiting for the normal approval cycle would cause unacceptable harm
Approval path Expedited approval, often from a smaller subset of the change board or a designated emergency authority, with full documentation and formal review completed retroactively after the fact
Example Emergency patching or isolating a system actively under active exploitation of a zero-day vulnerability

Exam Angle — Identifying Change Type from a Scenario

The exam tests this by describing a situation and asking which change type applies. Routine, done many times, low risk → standard. Planned, non-routine, time to go through full review → normal. Active incident, can't wait, needs to happen now → emergency. The key differentiator is urgency and precedent, not the technical complexity of the change itself — a technically simple change can still require normal review if it's non-routine, and a technically complex change can still be a standard change if it's been performed successfully many times with a pre-approved procedure.

Date and Time of Change

Every change request must specify exactly when the change will occur, accounting for two important constraints.

Change freeze A designated period during which no non-emergency changes are permitted at all — commonly enforced around a business's highest-stakes periods (e.g., a retailer freezing changes during its peak holiday shopping season) to eliminate any risk of a change-induced outage when the business can least afford one.
Maintenance windows Pre-scheduled, approved time blocks — typically during off-peak hours — set aside specifically for making changes with minimal impact on users, since fewer people are actively relying on the affected systems during that window.

Affected Systems/Impact

A documented accounting of every system, service, and user group the change will touch — directly or indirectly — so that reviewers can accurately weigh the change's risk and so that anyone impacted can be notified in advance. This is where a well-maintained CMDB (Objective 4.1) becomes directly useful: it reveals downstream dependencies that might not be obvious from the change alone.

Risk Analysis

Risk analysis is the formal evaluation of what could go wrong with the proposed change, how likely each failure scenario is, and how severe the consequences would be if it occurred. The output of a risk analysis is typically expressed as a risk level — a simplified rating (e.g., low, medium, high, or critical) that lets a change board quickly gauge how much scrutiny a given change warrants without re-deriving the full analysis themselves.

Risk Level Drives Everything Downstream

A change's assigned risk level typically determines how many people need to review it, whether it requires a maintenance window versus being safe to implement during business hours, and how thorough the rollback and backup plans need to be. Higher risk level does not mean a change is denied — it means the change gets more scrutiny and more safeguards proportional to what could go wrong.

Change Board Approvals

A change board (sometimes called a Change Advisory Board, or CAB) is a designated group of stakeholders — often representing IT, security, and affected business units — responsible for reviewing proposed normal changes and formally approving or rejecting them before implementation. The change board's review is where purpose, scope, risk analysis, and affected-systems impact all get weighed together against the organization's tolerance for risk at that time.

Implementation

The actual execution of the approved change, ideally carried out by the responsible staff member(s) named earlier, following whatever procedure was tested in the sandbox and documented in the request — with the rollback and backup plans staged and ready in case anything goes wrong during this step.

Peer Review

Peer review is a second qualified technician independently checking the change's plan (and often the actual implementation) before or as it goes live — catching mistakes, blind spots, or overlooked risks that the original implementer might have missed simply from being too close to their own work. Peer review is a lightweight but effective quality-control step distinct from the more formal change board approval.

End-User Acceptance

End-user acceptance confirms that the people who actually use the affected system are satisfied the change achieved its intended purpose and didn't introduce new problems from their perspective — closing the loop on the change. A change can be implemented flawlessly from a technical standpoint and still fail this step if it doesn't actually solve the user's underlying problem or introduces an unexpected workflow disruption.

The Complete Change Management Sequence
01
Request submitted via a standardized change request form, stating the purpose and scope of the change.
02
Classification as a standard, normal, or emergency change, which determines how much review the request requires.
03
Risk analysis performed, affected systems/impact documented, and a risk level assigned.
04
Sandbox testing of the proposed change in an isolated environment, surfacing problems before they reach production.
05
Change board review and approval (for normal changes), or expedited approval (for emergency changes); standard changes skip this step since they're pre-approved.
06
Scheduling within an appropriate maintenance window, and confirmation that no change freeze is currently in effect.
07
Backup plan and rollback plan finalized and staged, with responsible staff members confirmed and ready.
08
Implementation of the change by the responsible staff member(s), with peer review confirming the plan and execution.
09
End-user acceptance confirms the change achieved its purpose without introducing new problems, formally closing the change.

REFERENCEQuick Reference Cards

Change Type Decision Guide

  • Done many times before, pre-approved procedure — standard change
  • Planned, non-routine, time for full review — normal change
  • Active incident, can't wait for full review — emergency change
  • Emergency changes still get documented — just after the fact, not before

Safety Net Elements (Section 1)

  • "How do we undo this?" — rollback plan
  • "What if rollback itself fails?" — backup plan
  • "Did we test it first, safely?" — sandbox testing
  • "Who owns this if it breaks?" — responsible staff members

Timing Concepts

  • No changes allowed at all right now — change freeze
  • Scheduled low-impact time slot for changes — maintenance window
  • Freeze applies even to most emergency-adjacent work — exceptions are rare and reviewed carefully

Who Signs Off, and When

  • Before implementation (normal change) — change board approval
  • During/around implementation — peer review
  • After implementation, closing the loop — end-user acceptance
  • Standard changes — skip case-by-case board approval entirely

Final Exam Reminders

A rollback plan must exist before implementation begins — a scenario describing a technician improvising a way to undo a failed change mid-incident is describing a missing rollback plan, not a successful one.

Standard changes are pre-approved and routine; normal changes require full review; emergency changes are urgent and documented after the fact — the differentiator is urgency and precedent, not technical complexity.

A change freeze blocks non-emergency changes entirely for a defined period (e.g., a retail change freeze during the holiday season), while a maintenance window is simply the scheduled, low-impact time slot changes are normally implemented in — don't confuse the two.

Risk level is the summarized output of a risk analysis and typically drives how much review, how many approvers, and how much rollback/backup preparation a change requires.

Change board approval and peer review are two distinct checks — the change board approves whether the change should happen at all; peer review checks whether the plan/execution itself is sound. A scenario can require both.

End-user acceptance is the final step, not implementation — a change isn't considered complete just because it was technically executed successfully; it's complete once the affected users confirm it actually solved their problem.

Sandbox testing happens before implementation, in an environment separate from production — testing directly in production defeats the purpose and is exactly the kind of shortcut the exam expects you to flag as a process failure.
