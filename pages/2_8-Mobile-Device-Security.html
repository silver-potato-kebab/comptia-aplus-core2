OVERVIEWIntroduction — The Mobile Security Challenge

Mobile devices — smartphones and tablets — present a unique security profile that differs significantly from workstations. They are always with the user, always connected, always on, and always carrying sensitive data. They leave the organization's physical perimeter constantly. They run applications from third-party stores with varying quality controls. They mix personal and corporate data. And they get lost — a lot.

The combination of portability, persistent connectivity, and personal/corporate data mixing makes mobile devices one of the most difficult platforms to secure effectively. Objective 2.8 covers the layered controls available to a technician to reduce the risk of data breach from a lost, stolen, or compromised mobile device.

Physical Theft
Stolen phone; unauthorized data access
Malicious Apps
Trojanized apps; unofficial stores
Network Attacks
Evil twin; unsecured public Wi-Fi
Policy Violations
Jailbreak; corporate data on personal apps

Android vs. iOS Platform Differences

The two dominant mobile platforms — Android (Google) and iOS/iPadOS (Apple) — implement security differently. iOS has a closed ecosystem with strict App Store vetting, hardware-backed encryption enabled by default, and limited sideloading. Android is more open, supports multiple app stores, and security varies across device manufacturers and Android versions. Most security controls in this objective apply to both platforms, but the implementation path differs. MDM solutions handle both platforms through their respective APIs.

SECTION 1Hardening Techniques

Device Encryption

Device encryption on mobile platforms protects data stored on the device's internal storage and (where applicable) the external SD card. Like BitLocker on a Windows laptop, device encryption ensures that if the phone is stolen, the data is cryptographically inaccessible without the correct authentication credentials.

Mobile Device Encryption iOS + Android
iOS (iPhone/iPad)
Encryption is always on and cannot be disabled on iOS. Every iPhone and iPad since the 3GS uses hardware-backed AES-256 encryption for all stored data. The encryption keys are derived from the device's unique hardware UID (unique identifier burned into the processor) combined with the user's passcode. When a device is wiped, the master keys are discarded, making all data cryptographically unrecoverable without physically breaking the hardware. This is why Apple can truthfully say they cannot unlock a seized iPhone — the key is partially derived from hardware they do not control.
Android
Modern Android devices (Android 10+ on most devices, Android 6+ on devices certified for it) also enable file-based encryption (FBE) by default. FBE encrypts files individually with keys derived from the user's PIN/password and the device hardware, providing encrypted-at-rest protection for all data. Older Android devices used full-disk encryption (FDE) which was less efficient. The encryption is only as strong as the PIN or password protecting the key — a 4-digit PIN provides very weak key derivation. Longer PINs and alphanumeric passwords strengthen the encryption.
SD card encryption
On Android devices with microSD card slots, external storage may not be encrypted by default. Android offers an "adoptable storage" mode that encrypts the SD card and ties it to the specific device (the card becomes unusable in other devices). If an SD card is removed from a device in non-adoptable mode, its contents are readable on any card reader. Enterprises using Android devices should enforce SD card encryption via MDM or require adoptable storage for any device with removable storage.
Encryption effectiveness
Mobile encryption is only as effective as the screen lock protecting it. A device with strong encryption but no screen lock (or a swipe-only lock) is effectively unencrypted against a motivated attacker — they just turn on the screen to access data. Encryption + strong screen lock is the required combination. Encryption protects against someone physically removing the storage chip; the screen lock protects against someone who picks up the running device.

Screen Locks

A screen lock is the authentication mechanism that protects a mobile device when it is idle — requiring the user to prove identity before gaining access to the running OS and data. Screen locks are the primary protection against unauthorized access to a found or stolen device and the foundation on which device encryption effectiveness rests.

Facial RecognitionHIGH SECURITY
—Mechanism: Infrared 3D face mapping (Face ID) or 2D camera-based recognition
—Apple Face ID: TrueDepth IR + dot projector; 3D; extremely spoof-resistant; 1 in 1,000,000 false accept rate
—Android face unlock: Varies widely by device; many use 2D camera (less secure); premium devices use 3D IR
—Falls back to: PIN if face not recognized (illness, mask, glasses change)
—Limitation: Cannot use in some environments (mask mandates); requires specific hardware
FingerprintHIGH SECURITY
—Mechanism: Capacitive, optical, or ultrasonic fingerprint scanner reads ridge patterns
—Under-display optical: Common on Android flagships; adequate security
—Under-display ultrasonic: Samsung Qualcomm ultrasonic; more secure; works with wet fingers
—Side-mounted: Common on mid-range; fast and reliable
—Limitation: Gloves, wet hands, certain injuries; can be compelled legally in some jurisdictions unlike passcode
PIN CodeMEDIUM-HIGH
—4-digit PIN: 10,000 combinations; weak — cracked in minutes with tools if lockout not enforced
—6-digit PIN: 1,000,000 combinations; significantly better; Apple default recommendation
—Alphanumeric passcode: Full keyboard; strongest knowledge-based option; use with password manager
—Protects encryption key: The PIN contributes to the key derivation for device encryption — longer PIN = stronger encryption
—Cannot be compelled: 5th Amendment protection in U.S. (vs. biometrics which can be compelled)
PatternMEDIUM
—Mechanism: Android only; draw a pattern connecting dots on a 3×3 grid
—Theory: 389,112 possible patterns of length 4–9
—Reality: People use predictable L-, Z-, or N-shaped patterns; effective combinations far fewer
—Smudge attack: Finger oil left on screen often reveals the pattern on a reflective surface; wipe screen after use
—Better than swipe: Still provides some protection; preferable to no lock at all
SwipeNO SECURITY
—Mechanism: Swipe a finger across the screen to unlock — no authentication
—Security value: Zero. Prevents accidental unlocking in pocket only. Anyone can unlock it.
—Use case: Personal devices with no sensitive data; never acceptable for corporate use
—MDM enforcement: Corporate profiles should prevent swipe as the sole screen lock; require minimum PIN
—Exam note: Swipe is included in the exam objectives specifically to contrast with more secure methods
NoneCRITICAL RISK
—No screen lock at all means no authentication barrier; immediate full device access
—Encryption useless: If device is unlocked (running), encryption protects nothing — the OS has already decrypted everything in memory
—Never acceptable on a device that accesses corporate data
—MDM must enforce: Compliance check; non-compliant devices blocked from corporate resources

Exam Focus — Screen Lock Security Rankings

From strongest to weakest: Biometric (Face ID/3D IR fingerprint) + PIN backup → Alphanumeric passcode → 6-digit PIN → 4-digit PIN → Pattern → Swipe (no security) → No lock (critical risk). The exam may ask which method provides the least security (swipe or none) or which biometric is most secure (Face ID with IR depth sensing).

Configuration Profiles

A configuration profile is a signed XML file (on iOS/iPadOS, called a mobileconfig file; on Android, implemented via MDM policy) that enforces specific settings and restrictions on a mobile device. Configuration profiles are the mechanism by which MDM systems push policies to enrolled devices. They represent the "how" of enterprise mobile policy enforcement.

Configuration Profiles — What They Control MDM Deployed
Security settings
Minimum passcode length and complexity; maximum screen lock idle time; maximum number of failed passcode attempts before wipe; require passcode change every N days; disable biometrics if required (high-security environments may require PIN-only for auditing). These settings are enforced — the user cannot change them to be less secure while the profile is installed.
Restrictions
Disable camera (secure facilities, classified environments); prevent screenshots; disable iCloud/Google Drive sync (data sovereignty); restrict App Store to approved apps only; prevent sideloading (Android); disable AirDrop/NFC for data transfer; prevent installation of apps outside the corporate app catalog; lock down Siri/voice assistant; restrict browser to approved browsers.
Network configuration
Pre-configure Wi-Fi credentials (SSID, security type, certificate for 802.1X); force VPN "always on" for all traffic on unrecognized networks; configure email accounts (Exchange, Gmail, IMAP); deploy certificates for Wi-Fi authentication and email signing/encryption. The user receives working configurations without needing to know or enter credentials — and without being able to change or remove them.
Managed apps
Deploy corporate apps silently (no user action required); separate managed (corporate) apps from personal apps in a containerized workspace; enforce data boundaries between managed and unmanaged apps (e.g., prevent corporate email data from being pasted into personal apps); require app-level PIN separate from device PIN; enable mobile app management (MAM) features.
Compliance enforcement
Define what constitutes a compliant device (minimum OS version, no jailbreak/root, required apps installed, encryption enabled). MDM continuously evaluates devices against compliance rules. A non-compliant device is automatically blocked from corporate resources (email, SharePoint, corporate Wi-Fi) until the issue is resolved. The user receives a notification explaining what needs to be corrected.

SECTION 2Patch Management

Mobile devices, like all computing platforms, require regular security patches to address vulnerabilities in the OS and installed applications. Mobile patch management is complicated by the diversity of hardware manufacturers (Android especially), carrier involvement in update distribution, and users' tendency to defer or ignore update prompts.

Mobile Patch Management iOS + Android
OS Updates — iOS
Apple provides iOS updates directly to devices without carrier intermediation. Updates are available simultaneously to all supported devices. Apple maintains relatively long support windows (5–6 years for major devices). Users can apply updates via Settings → General → Software Update. MDM can enforce a minimum iOS version — devices below the minimum are quarantined until updated. MDM can also delay updates by up to 90 days (in supervised mode) to allow enterprise testing before forcing the update on managed devices.
OS Updates — Android
Android updates are fragmented — Google provides the core Android update (including monthly security patches via Project Mainline), but device manufacturers and sometimes carriers must adapt and distribute it. A Samsung Galaxy device's update schedule is different from a Pixel's. Google Pixel devices receive updates fastest (5 years of Android/security updates). Enterprise Android deployments should standardize on devices with predictable update schedules. MDM can enforce minimum patch levels and quarantine out-of-date devices.
Application Updates — App Stores
Apps installed from the App Store (iOS) or Google Play Store (Android) receive security patches via app updates. Enable automatic app updates — most vulnerabilities in popular apps (browsers, email clients, PDF viewers) are patched quickly after discovery; staying current is the most effective protection. On iOS: Settings → App Store → App Updates toggle. On Android: Play Store → Settings → Network preferences → Auto-update apps. MDM can enforce minimum app version requirements for managed apps.
Enterprise patch enforcement
MDM platforms can require minimum OS and security patch levels for device compliance. Devices that fail to update within a defined window lose access to corporate resources. For iOS in supervised mode, admins can defer updates to a testing window and then force the update. For Android, enterprise device management (Android Enterprise) provides similar controls on fully managed devices. The challenge remains with BYOD — users may defer updates; the MDM compliance policy and conditional access enforcement is the lever.
End of Support risks
A mobile device that no longer receives OS updates (out of support) accumulates permanent vulnerabilities that can never be patched — the mobile equivalent of an EOL workstation OS. MDM compliance policies should define a minimum supported OS version and flag or block devices running versions that are no longer receiving security updates. The safest remediation is device replacement.

SECTION 3Endpoint Security Software

Mobile endpoint security software extends traditional endpoint protection concepts to smartphones and tablets. The nature of mobile security differs from desktop: iOS's sandboxed app model and closed ecosystem makes traditional AV scanning largely unnecessary (and architecturally impossible), while Android's more open ecosystem makes malware detection more relevant.

Mobile Endpoint Security Protection Layers
Antivirus / Anti-malware
Android: Traditional malware (Trojans, spyware, adware) does exist in the Android ecosystem, primarily distributed via unofficial app stores and sideloaded APKs. Mobile AV products (Malwarebytes Mobile, Lookout, Bitdefender Mobile Security) scan installed apps against malware signatures, detect malicious APKs before installation, and monitor for behavior anomalies. Google Play Protect — built into Android — provides basic malware scanning of installed apps automatically.

iOS: Apple's sandboxing model prevents apps from accessing other apps' data, making traditional virus/malware behavior architecturally impossible. AV products on iOS cannot scan other apps or system files. iOS security products focus instead on network threat detection, phishing protection, and device posture assessment rather than traditional AV scanning.
Content Filtering
Content filtering on mobile devices restricts access to categories of websites or applications — protecting users from phishing sites, malware-hosting URLs, inappropriate content, and productivity-draining sites. Implementation options:

DNS-based filtering — configure the device to use a content-filtering DNS resolver (Cisco Umbrella, CleanBrowsing, OpenDNS) that blocks resolution of known malicious or policy-violating domains. Works at the network level; filters all apps, not just browsers.

VPN-based filtering — route all device traffic through the organization's proxy/content filter via always-on VPN. Most comprehensive; allows consistent policy enforcement regardless of which network the device is connected to.

MDM app restrictions — whitelist approved browsers and require managed browser (Microsoft Edge managed, Intune Managed Browser) that enforces web filtering policies.
Mobile Threat Defense (MTD)
A more advanced category than basic AV — Mobile Threat Defense (MTD) solutions (Lookout, Zimperium, Microsoft Defender for Endpoint mobile) provide: network threat detection (identify evil twin APs, SSL stripping, MITM attacks), device posture assessment (detect jailbreak/root, OS manipulation, suspicious configurations), app analysis (identify risky app behaviors), and integration with MDM/Zero Trust conditional access. MTD integrates with MDM compliance frameworks — a device that detects an active network attack is automatically quarantined from corporate resources until the threat is resolved.

SECTION 4Locator Applications, Remote Wipe, and Remote Backup

Lost and stolen mobile devices are an ever-present reality. The controls in this section allow organizations and individuals to respond to a lost or stolen device after the fact — locating it, protecting data on it, and recovering data from it. These controls are the "what do we do now?" response plan that must be configured before a device is lost, not after.

Locator Applications

Device Location Services iOS + Android
Find My (iOS)
Apple's Find My network (formerly Find My iPhone) tracks the device's location using GPS, Wi-Fi positioning, and the crowdsourced Find My network (Bluetooth pings from nearby Apple devices). Accessible from icloud.com or the Find My app on another Apple device. Find My must be enabled in Settings → [Apple ID] → Find My → Find My iPhone before loss. Requires iCloud account. The Activation Lock feature (tied to Find My) prevents a wiped iPhone from being activated without the original Apple ID — making stolen iPhones significantly less valuable to thieves.
Find My Device (Android)
Google's Find My Device (findmydevice.google.com) locates a device logged in with a Google account, with location services enabled. Requires: location enabled on device, device signed into Google account, device online (Wi-Fi or mobile data). The Find My Device network (Android 6+) also uses a crowdsourced network of nearby Android devices. Accessible via the Find My Device app on another device or the Google web interface.
MDM-based location
MDM platforms provide centralized device location independent of the device's native location service. Enterprise MDM (Intune, Jamf) can show the last known location of enrolled devices on a map. For corporate-owned devices, location tracking is fully justifiable and enforceable. For BYOD devices, location tracking raises significant privacy concerns — employees typically object to their employer tracking their personal device's location 24/7. Most enterprise MDM policies track only corporate-owned devices or limit BYOD location access to the moment of a theft report.
Location prerequisites
Location services must be enabled before the device is lost. A device in airplane mode or with GPS/location disabled cannot be located. The device must have battery charge and network connectivity. These prerequisites must be enforced via MDM compliance policy for corporate devices — a device with location services disabled should be flagged as non-compliant, because in the event of loss it cannot be located or remotely wiped.

Remote Wipe

A remote wipe is a command sent to a lost or stolen device that erases its data, protecting the organization from unauthorized access to corporate information. Remote wipe must be configured before loss — the device must be enrolled in MDM or connected to a platform that supports remote commands (iCloud, Google, Exchange ActiveSync).

Remote Wipe Options Execute Only When Certain
Full device wipe
Erases all data on the device — both corporate and personal — and restores it to factory default settings. The most destructive option; appropriate for: corporate-owned devices confirmed lost or stolen; devices belonging to employees who have been terminated; devices with confirmed malware compromise. On iOS: triggered from iCloud Find My or MDM; erases everything and disables Activation Lock if triggered by MDM. On Android: triggered from Find My Device or MDM; returns to factory state.
Selective wipe
Removes only corporate data and managed apps while leaving personal photos, contacts, and apps intact. The appropriate option for BYOD devices — the organization has no right to destroy the employee's personal data. A selective wipe removes the MDM profile, corporate email accounts, managed app data, configuration profiles, and certificates. Personal data is untouched. Available on iOS (MDM unenrollment wipes managed content) and Android (Android Enterprise work profile removal wipes the work profile container, leaving personal profile intact).
When to initiate wipe
The decision to wipe a device should be deliberate and documented. Common triggers: device confirmed lost or stolen by the user; employee termination (corporate-owned devices immediately; BYOD selective wipe); device not reporting compliance for extended period (suspected compromise or abandonment); user request when switching to a new device. A wipe should not be initiated until attempts to locate the device have been exhausted — a wipe makes the device unlocatable.
Remote wipe limitations
Remote wipe only works if: the device has network connectivity (Wi-Fi or cellular) to receive the wipe command, and the device has battery charge. A device in airplane mode, powered off, or with a dead battery cannot receive the wipe command until it reconnects. The wipe command is queued and will execute when the device reconnects. This is why device encryption (which does not require connectivity) provides protection when remote wipe cannot.

Remote Backup Applications

Remote backup automatically copies device data to a cloud service, ensuring that data is recoverable after a device is lost, stolen, wiped, or damaged. Remote backup is the complement to remote wipe — it ensures that wiping a device to protect data does not also mean permanently losing that data.

iCloud Backup (iOS) Automatically backs up: app data, Apple Watch backups, device settings, Home screen and app organization, iMessages/SMS, photos and videos (via iCloud Photos), purchase history, ringtones, and Visual Voicemail. Backups are encrypted in iCloud. Runs automatically when device is connected to power, locked, and on Wi-Fi. Requires sufficient iCloud storage. Configure via: Settings → [Apple ID] → iCloud → iCloud Backup → Back Up Now (on-demand) or toggle automatic backup on.
Google One / Android Backup Android backup copies: app data, call history, contacts, device settings, SMS messages, and photos/videos (Google Photos). Backs up to Google Drive/Google One. Configure via: Settings → System → Backup → Back up to Google Drive. Google Photos provides unlimited photo/video backup (quality-limited) independent of the system backup. Consistency of what is backed up varies more across Android manufacturers than iOS.
Enterprise backup considerations For corporate data, MDM platforms can enforce that corporate app data is backed up to enterprise storage rather than personal cloud accounts. Microsoft Intune's App Protection Policies can restrict corporate app data backup to approved corporate storage (OneDrive for Business, SharePoint) and prevent backup of corporate data to personal iCloud or Google accounts — a DLP control within the backup mechanism.
Backup before wipe When a device is being intentionally wiped (not an emergency remote wipe after theft), confirm that a recent backup exists before initiating the wipe. For BYOD devices undergoing a selective wipe, remind the user to verify their personal data is backed up before the IT team initiates the wipe — even though the selective wipe should not affect personal data, backup is prudent insurance against unexpected outcomes.

SECTION 5Failed Login Attempts Restrictions

Limiting the number of consecutive failed authentication attempts before taking protective action defends against in-person brute-force attacks — someone with physical possession of a locked device systematically trying PINs or patterns. This control is distinct from network account lockout (covered in 2.7) and applies specifically to the mobile device's local authentication.

Failed Authentication Limits iOS + Android
iOS failed attempt limit
iOS implements a graduated lockout that escalates with each failure:

After 6 failures: 1-minute lockout
After 7 failures: 5-minute lockout
After 8 failures: 15-minute lockout
After 9 failures: 60-minute lockout
After 10 failures: Device disabled ("iPhone is disabled") or wiped

The "Erase Data after 10 failed attempts" option in Settings → Face ID & Passcode is disabled by default but can be enabled for high-security environments. MDM can enforce this setting and set the failure threshold lower (minimum 3 attempts, maximum 11).
Android failed attempt limit
Android's response to failed PIN/pattern attempts varies by device and Android version. Generally: after several failures, Android shows a cooldown timer (30-second to multi-minute delays before retry). After a configurable number of failures (set by MDM or device settings), the device may require the Google account password to unlock, or a full factory reset. Google-managed devices through Android Enterprise can enforce wipe after N failed attempts via MDM policy.
MDM enforcement
MDM policy (iOS: Passcode Payload; Android: Password Policy) sets the maximum number of failed attempts before wipe across all enrolled devices — overriding or supplementing the device's default behavior. For high-security environments, this can be set as low as 5–6 attempts. The MDM-enforced limit applies regardless of what the user has configured in their device settings (MDM settings take precedence over user-accessible settings for managed devices).
Biometric + PIN fallback
When biometric authentication fails too many times (typically 5 failures for Face ID, 5 for fingerprint), the device requires the passcode/PIN instead of continuing biometric retries. The PIN fallback is what ultimately prevents brute-force — it applies the failed attempt counter and lockout/wipe enforcement. This ensures that an attacker who defeats biometric authentication still cannot brute-force the PIN without triggering the lockout policy.

SECTION 6Policies and Procedures

Mobile Device Management (MDM)

Mobile Device Management (MDM) is the software infrastructure that enables central management, monitoring, configuration, and enforcement of security policies across a fleet of mobile devices. MDM is the enabling technology for nearly every enterprise mobile security control — without MDM, policies exist only as user guidelines that individuals can choose to follow or ignore.

MDM Platform Capabilities Enterprise MDM
Policy enforcement
MDM pushes configuration profiles (iOS) or device policies (Android Enterprise) that enforce security requirements directly on the device — users cannot change MDM-managed settings. Examples: minimum 6-character PIN enforced; camera disabled in the MDM profile; VPN always-on for all traffic; approved app list enforced. The user may see that a setting is controlled by their organization and cannot modify it — a visible lock icon appears next to managed settings on iOS.
Application management
Over-the-air (OTA) app deployment: push required corporate apps to enrolled devices silently (no user action). App blocking: prevent installation of blacklisted apps. Approved app catalog: provide a curated in-house app store. Managed app configurations: pre-configure app settings (Exchange server address, SharePoint URL, VPN configurations) so users don't need to manually configure corporate apps. App license management: deploy and revoke volume-purchased app licenses.
Compliance monitoring
MDM continuously evaluates each device against the organization's compliance baseline. Compliance checks include: minimum OS version, minimum security patch level, encryption enabled, screen lock set, no jailbreak/root detected, required apps installed, prohibited apps absent. Non-compliant devices are flagged in the MDM console. Via integration with conditional access (Azure AD / Intune), non-compliant devices are automatically blocked from corporate email, SharePoint, Teams, and other services until compliance is restored.
Inventory and reporting
MDM maintains a real-time inventory of all enrolled devices: device model, serial number, OS version, installed apps, last check-in time, compliance status, and assigned user. This inventory supports security incident response (identify which devices access sensitive data), compliance reporting (audit evidence), procurement planning (identify aging devices nearing end of support), and capacity management.
Remote actions
In addition to policy enforcement, MDM provides on-demand remote actions: remote lock (lock immediately regardless of idle timeout), remote wipe (full or selective), remote passcode reset (iOS — remove the passcode so IT can access a device), location query (GPS coordinates), push notification (display message on device), and unenroll device (remove MDM profile and all managed content). Enterprise MDM examples: Microsoft Intune, Jamf (Apple-focused), VMware Workspace ONE, Ivanti MobileIron.

BYOD vs. Corporate-Owned Devices

The choice between allowing employees to use their personal devices (BYOD) or providing organization-owned devices (corporate-owned, also called COPE — Corporate Owned, Personally Enabled) is one of the most consequential mobile security policy decisions an organization makes. Each model has distinct security capabilities, management levels, privacy implications, and cost profiles.

BYOD — Bring Your Own Device
Corporate-Owned (COPE)
Ownership: Employee owns device
Ownership: Organization owns device
Cost to org: Lower (no hardware purchase)
Cost to org: Higher (purchase, replace, manage)
Employee privacy: High concern — MDM may see personal data; employees often resist full MDM enrollment
Employee privacy: Lower expectation — employee knows org owns device; work profile vs. personal may still be separated
MDM scope: Limited — typically work profile (Android) or MAM (iOS) to avoid personal data visibility
MDM scope: Full device management; all settings configurable; no privacy limitations

Best for: High-security environments; employees who handle sensitive data; environments requiring full policy control

Exam Focus — BYOD vs. Corporate Owned Key Differences

The exam tests three key BYOD vs. corporate distinctions: (1) Wipe scope: BYOD = selective wipe only; Corporate = full wipe acceptable. (2) MDM scope: BYOD = limited (work profile/container); Corporate = full device management. (3) Privacy: BYOD = significant employee privacy considerations; Corporate = minimal privacy expectation for work use. A scenario asking "a company lost an employee's personal phone containing corporate data — what remote action should they take?" → selective wipe (not full wipe, as it is a personal device).

Profile Security Requirements

Profile security requirements are the minimum security standards a mobile device must meet to be enrolled in MDM and maintain access to corporate resources. These requirements are defined in the MDM compliance policy and enforced through conditional access integration. They ensure that devices in the corporate environment meet a defined security baseline — neither the hardware nor the user's choices can create a security gap that compromises corporate data.

Common Profile Security Requirements MDM Compliance Policy
Screen lock requirements
Minimum passcode length (typically 6+ characters); require alphanumeric passcode for high-security environments; maximum screen lock timeout (5–15 minutes); disallow swipe-only unlock; maximum failed attempts before wipe (6–10). These settings are pushed via configuration profile — users cannot set a weaker passcode than the policy minimum while the profile is installed. On iOS, if a user removes the profile, they lose access to corporate resources.
OS version requirements
Define the minimum supported iOS or Android version. Devices running EOL or outdated OS versions are blocked from corporate access. The compliance policy automatically re-evaluates compliance when OS updates are detected — a device that updates to a compliant OS version is automatically granted access again without IT intervention.
Jailbreak / root detection
Jailbreaking (iOS) — exploiting vulnerabilities to remove Apple's restrictions, allowing unsigned app installation and root access. Bypasses the security model entirely. Rooting (Android) — gaining root (superuser) access, bypassing manufacturer restrictions. Both fundamentally undermine the device's security model and make enterprise security controls ineffective. MDM compliance checks detect common jailbreak/root indicators. A jailbroken/rooted device should be immediately flagged as non-compliant and blocked from corporate resources.
Encryption requirement
Compliance policy should require device encryption enabled. On modern iOS devices, encryption is always on (non-configurable), so this check is automatically satisfied. On Android, some older devices or custom ROMs may not have encryption enabled — MDM verifies encryption status via the Android management API and flags devices without encryption as non-compliant.
Approved device types
Organizations can restrict enrollment to specific device models, manufacturers, or iOS/Android versions. Example: allow only Apple devices running iOS 16+, or only Samsung Knox-enabled devices for Android. This ensures hardware security features (Secure Enclave, Samsung Knox) are present and functioning. Unapproved device types are rejected at enrollment.

High-Probability Exam Scenarios

Scenario 1: An employee's corporate iPhone is stolen — what two actions should IT take? → Remote wipe (full — corporate-owned) AND locate via Find My.
Scenario 2: An employee's personal Android phone with corporate email is lost — what is the appropriate remote action? → Selective wipe only — removes corporate data without erasing personal photos and apps.
Scenario 3: A user has no screen lock on their BYOD device that accesses corporate email — what should happen? → MDM compliance check fails; conditional access blocks corporate resource access until a compliant screen lock is set.
Scenario 4: A device shows as jailbroken in the MDM console — what should happen? → Mark non-compliant; block corporate access; investigate; full wipe if corporate-owned.
Scenario 5: A company wants to prevent corporate data from being copied from Outlook to personal apps on BYOD iPhones → MDM App Protection Policy (MAM) with managed app restrictions on cut/copy/paste between managed and unmanaged apps.
Scenario 6: Which screen lock method provides the lowest security? → Swipe (no authentication required).
Scenario 7: A company is rolling out Android devices in a high-security environment — which screen lock should be required? → Minimum 6-digit PIN enforced via MDM compliance policy; biometric as secondary method.
Scenario 8: An employee is leaving the company and had corporate apps on their personal iPhone — what happens during BYOD offboarding? → Selective wipe removes corporate apps and data; personal content untouched.
