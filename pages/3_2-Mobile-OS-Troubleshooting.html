Troubleshoot Common Mobile OS and Application Issues

App Failures OS Updates Battery & Performance Connectivity Screen Rotation

Mobile devices — smartphones and tablets running iOS/iPadOS or Android — are front-line productivity tools in modern workplaces, and troubleshooting them is a core A+ technician skill. Mobile OS troubleshooting differs from desktop troubleshooting in important ways: you cannot run arbitrary diagnostic commands, access is mediated through app stores and OS-level permissions, and many fixes involve gestures and settings menus rather than command-line tools. The symptoms in this objective are the ones users most commonly bring to help desk: apps that refuse to open, batteries that drain too fast, Wi-Fi that drops, and screens that won't rotate.

For each symptom, a technician must know the common causes on both major platforms, the correct sequence of diagnostic steps, and when to escalate from simple fixes to more drastic measures like factory reset. The exam presents these as scenario questions — a user describes a specific symptom on a specific platform and you must identify the most appropriate next step.

Platform Scope — Android and iOS/iPadOS

Every symptom in this objective applies to both Android and iOS/iPadOS, but the exact navigation path to the fix differs by platform. The exam is platform-aware — scenarios may specify Android or iOS. The platform comparison strips throughout this guide show where the key settings live on each. iOS refers to iPhone, iPadOS to iPad (functionally identical for these purposes); Android applies to Samsung, Google Pixel, and other Android-based devices.

Section AMobile Troubleshooting Foundations

Before examining individual symptoms, a technician must understand the key interventions available on mobile devices. Unlike desktop Windows, mobile OS troubleshooting relies on a smaller but highly effective toolkit of escalating actions. Most issues resolve at steps 1–3; steps 4–5 are for persistent or severe problems.

1
Force-close the app — swipe away the app from the app switcher (recent apps). Clears the running process and any in-memory state without rebooting the device. First fix for any app-specific issue.
2
Restart the device — a full power cycle clears all running processes, RAM, and temporary system state. Resolves the majority of transient software issues including random reboots, app crashes, connectivity glitches, and sluggish performance. Should be the second action for almost any mobile issue.
3
Clear app cache / data — on Android: Settings → Apps → [app] → Storage → Clear Cache (removes temporary files) or Clear Data (resets the app to fresh-install state, deletes local data). On iOS: delete and reinstall the app (iOS does not expose a separate cache-clear option). Resolves app corruption without uninstalling on Android.
4
Uninstall and reinstall the app — downloads a fresh copy from the app store. Resolves corrupt installations, failed updates, and app-specific permission issues. User data stored server-side (cloud apps) is preserved; locally stored data is lost.
5
Factory reset (last resort) — wipes all user data and apps, restoring the device to out-of-box state. Resolves persistent OS-level issues that survive all other interventions. Requires full backup first. Android: Settings → General Management → Reset → Factory Reset. iOS: Settings → General → Transfer or Reset iPhone → Erase All Content and Settings.
Android Key Locations
App info: Settings → Apps → [app]
Clear cache: App info → Storage → Clear Cache
App permissions: App info → Permissions
Storage full: Settings → Storage
Developer options: Settings → About → tap Build Number ×7
Safe Mode: Hold power → long-press Power Off

Section BApplication Failures

Application Fails to Launch

Application Fails to Launch
App icon tapped but nothing opens, or app opens briefly and returns to home screen immediately
Common
Most common cause
Corrupt install or cached state
First fix
Force-close, then restart device
Escalation
Clear cache/data → Reinstall
Immediate triage
When an app tapped does not open or flashes and immediately returns to the home screen, start with the basics: force-close the app via the app switcher, then restart the device. Most launch failures are caused by a previous session crashing and leaving the app in a bad in-memory state — a restart clears this completely. If the app still fails to launch after restart, the issue is more persistent.
Insufficient storage
Insufficient storage is a leading cause of app launch failures on both platforms. Apps require free storage not just to install but to run — they write temporary files, caches, and logs during operation. A device with less than 500 MB of free storage may fail to launch apps that need to write to disk during startup. Android: Settings → Storage — check free space. Clear cached app data, delete unused apps, or move media to external storage. iOS: Settings → General → iPhone Storage — identifies large apps, cached data, and allows offloading apps (removes the app but keeps its data, freeing storage while preserving the ability to re-download).
Corrupt app installation
A failed update or interrupted download can leave an app in a corrupt state that prevents launch. On Android: Settings → Apps → [app] → Storage → Clear Cache first; if that fails, Clear Data, then try launching. If still broken, uninstall and reinstall from the Play Store. On iOS: there is no separate cache-clear option — go directly to Settings → General → iPhone Storage → [app] → Delete App, then reinstall from the App Store. Re-download ensures a fresh, uncorrupted installation.
Permissions not granted
Some apps crash on launch rather than prompting for permissions when a required permission was previously denied. Android: Settings → Apps → [app] → Permissions — review and grant required permissions (Camera, Location, Microphone, etc.). iOS: Settings → Privacy & Security → [permission type] → verify the app is listed and enabled. Alternatively: Settings → [app name] → review all permission toggles. Granting the required permission and relaunching often resolves immediate-crash-on-launch behavior.
OS/app incompatibility
An app may fail to launch because it requires a newer OS version than what is installed, or — less commonly — a recent OS update broke compatibility with an older app version. Check the app's listing in the Play Store or App Store for minimum OS requirements. If the OS is below the required version, update the OS (if the device supports it) or accept that the app cannot be used on that device. Conversely, if an app broke after an OS update, check whether a newer app version is available or whether the developer has acknowledged the issue.

Application Fails to Close / Crashes

Application Fails to Close / Crashes
App freezes, becomes unresponsive, or terminates unexpectedly mid-use
Common
Frozen / hung app
When an app stops responding to input (taps, swipes), it is frozen or hung. The OS has not yet killed it because it is still technically running — it has simply stopped processing the event queue. Resolution: force-close via the app switcher — swipe up from the bottom (iPhone/iPad without home button) or press the recents/overview button (Android), then swipe the frozen app off the screen. This sends a termination signal to the app process. If the touchscreen itself is unresponsive, perform a force restart: on iPhone 8+/iOS devices, quickly press Volume Up, Volume Down, then hold the Side button until the Apple logo appears. On Android: hold the power button for 10–15 seconds (model-dependent).
Random / recurring crashes
Apps that crash repeatedly (not just once after a restart) suggest a more persistent problem. Triage order: (1) Check for an app update in the store — the developer may have patched a known crash bug. (2) Clear cache/data (Android) or delete and reinstall (iOS). (3) Check available storage — apps crash when they cannot write temporary data. (4) Check available RAM — too many apps running simultaneously causes the OS to kill apps in the background, which may manifest as crash-like behavior when returning to the app. (5) If only one specific app crashes and others are fine, the issue is app-specific — contact the developer or report through the store.
App crashes after OS update
A major OS update can break app compatibility if the developer has not updated their app for the new OS version. Symptoms: apps that worked before the update now crash immediately or during specific operations. Check the app store for a pending app update — the developer typically releases compatibility fixes within days of a major OS release. If no update is available, the only option is to wait for the developer to release an update, use the app's web version if available, or roll back (iOS does not support OS rollback; Android rollback is complex and usually not end-user accessible).
Background vs. foreground crash
On both platforms, the OS may terminate background apps to free RAM for foreground activity — this is normal behavior, not a crash. If a user reports that "the app closes when I switch to another app," this is the OS performing memory management, not the app crashing. The user's data should be preserved if the app implements proper state restoration. Educate the user that this is expected. If the app is losing unsaved data when backgrounded, that is an app design issue — report it to the developer or advise the user to save frequently.

Application Fails to Update

Application Fails to Update
App update shows in the store but download stalls, errors out, or never completes
App Store / Play Store
Network connectivity
The most common cause of a failed app update is a network issue — the app store cannot reach the update server. Verify connectivity: open a website in the browser to confirm internet access. If on Wi-Fi, try switching to mobile data (or vice versa) — this quickly isolates whether the issue is network-specific. Large updates (>150 MB on iOS) require Wi-Fi by default on iOS — the user will see "This app requires a Wi-Fi connection to update" if they are on cellular. Disable and re-enable Wi-Fi to force reconnection to the AP if the connection appears connected but is not passing traffic.
Insufficient storage
App updates require free storage equal to (or slightly more than) the new version's size plus temporary space for the installer. If storage is critically low, the update download will fail or get stuck. Resolve: free up storage by deleting unused apps, photos (after backing up), or cached data before retrying the update. On iOS, the system sometimes automatically offloads unused apps to make room — this can be enabled in Settings → App Store → enable Offload Unused Apps.
Account authentication issues
App store updates are tied to the account that originally purchased/downloaded the app. If the store account credentials have changed, or if a corporate MDM profile has changed app ownership, the update may fail with an authentication error. Android (Play Store): ensure the correct Google account is signed in (Play Store → profile icon → verify account). iOS (App Store): tap the profile icon in the App Store → verify the Apple ID. If a pop-up asks for the original downloader's password and the app was installed by a different account, the device may need to re-sign in with that account or the app must be deleted and reinstalled under the current account.
Store cache corruption
The app store app itself can have a corrupt cache that prevents downloads. Android: Settings → Apps → Google Play Store → Storage → Clear Cache, then Clear Data. This resets Play Store without affecting installed apps or purchases. Restart the device and retry. iOS: there is no manual App Store cache clear; signing out of Apple ID (Settings → [your name] → Sign Out) and signing back in typically resolves authentication and cache-related App Store issues.
App no longer compatible
If the new version of an app requires a newer OS version than what the device runs, the update will not install. The App Store on iOS intelligently offers to install the latest compatible version instead. On Android, the Play Store may show the update as unavailable. The resolution is either to update the OS (if the device supports the required version) or to continue using the older, compatible version of the app — which means the user will not receive new features or security patches.

Application Fails to Install

Application Fails to Install
Download completes but installation fails, or download itself cannot start
Storage / Policy
Insufficient storage
Installation requires free storage for the app itself plus overhead. Both stores display the app's size before download. If storage is insufficient, the download either fails to start or fails during installation. Check storage: Android: Settings → Storage; iOS: Settings → General → iPhone Storage. Free up space before reattempting. iOS will display an alert: "Not Enough Storage — You can manage your storage in Settings."
MDM / enterprise restrictions
On MDM (Mobile Device Management)-managed devices, the IT administrator may have configured an app allowlist or blocklist. Users on managed devices may be prevented from installing apps that are not in the approved list, or all sideloading may be blocked. The error usually reads "Installation is blocked by your organization's policy." The resolution is an IT admin action — either approve the app in the MDM console (Intune, Jamf, VMware Workspace ONE) or push it to the device via the MDM-managed app catalog. The technician's role is to identify this as a policy issue and escalate to the MDM admin.
Android — unknown sources / sideloading
Android allows installation of apps from outside the Play Store — called sideloading — by installing an .apk file directly. By default, Android requires explicit permission to install from unknown sources. Android 8+: the permission is per-source-app — Settings → Apps → [browser or file manager used to download] → Install unknown apps → Allow. If a user is trying to install a legitimate enterprise app distributed as an APK (common with internal corporate apps), enable this setting for the specific downloader app only, install the APK, and then disable the setting again to minimize the attack surface of allowing unknown sources.
iOS — untrusted developer
On iOS, apps outside the App Store can only be installed via an enterprise distribution profile (for corporate apps) or TestFlight (for beta apps). When a user installs a corporate enterprise app for the first time, iOS displays "Untrusted Enterprise Developer." Resolution: Settings → General → VPN & Device Management → find the enterprise developer → Trust. This step must be performed after every device restore or enrollment. If the profile is expired or revoked by the developer, the app will not open — the IT admin must renew the enterprise developer certificate and redistribute the app.

Section CPerformance and OS Issues

Slow to Respond

Slow to Respond
Device feels laggy — delayed touch response, slow app launches, sluggish animations
Common Complaint
First action
Restart the device
Storage check
Under 10–15% free = performance impact
Battery impact
Degraded battery → CPU throttling
Too many background processes
Unlike desktop computers, mobile devices actively limit background processing — but this does not mean background apps have zero impact. On Android, apps with background services (music players, navigation apps, email sync, notification listeners) consume RAM and CPU continuously. Open the app switcher and close apps that don't need to be running. On Android, check which apps are actively running in the background: Settings → Battery → Battery Usage or Developer Options → Running Services. On iOS, the OS manages background apps aggressively and closing apps from the switcher is rarely necessary — Apple explicitly states that iOS handles memory automatically and force-closing apps may slow down relaunching them.
Insufficient storage
When device storage is nearly full, performance degrades significantly. Both Android and iOS use free storage space for virtual memory operations, system updates, and app temporary files. Rule of thumb: keep at least 10–15% of total storage free. A 64 GB device with less than 6–9 GB free will exhibit noticeable slowness. Resolution: delete unused apps, clear app caches, offload photos to cloud storage (Google Photos, iCloud), and remove downloaded media.
Battery health and CPU throttling
Lithium-ion batteries degrade over time — a battery at 70% health cannot deliver peak current. Both Apple and Android throttle CPU performance when peak power demand exceeds what the degraded battery can supply, to prevent unexpected shutdowns. This manifests as general system sluggishness that worsens over time as the battery degrades further. iOS: Settings → Battery → Battery Health & Charging — if capacity is below ~80%, throttling is active and battery replacement resolves the performance issue. Android: battery health varies by manufacturer — some Samsung devices show health in Settings → Battery → Battery Status; third-party apps like AccuBattery provide health estimates. Battery replacement on most modern smartphones requires professional service.
OS update pending
A downloaded but not-yet-installed OS update running background preparation tasks (decrypting update files, verifying integrity) can temporarily slow the device. Check for pending updates and install them — the temporary slowness during update preparation resolves after installation completes. Conversely, immediately after installing a major OS update, the device may be slow for 15–60 minutes while the OS re-indexes content, re-optimizes apps, and completes background setup tasks — this is normal and resolves on its own.
Malware (Android)
On Android, malware (particularly adware and cryptominers) can continuously consume CPU and RAM, causing persistent sluggishness. Signs: device is hot even at idle, battery drains rapidly at rest, performance does not improve after restart. Investigate in Settings → Battery → Battery Usage — any unfamiliar app consuming high background CPU is suspicious. Install a reputable mobile security scanner (Malwarebytes, Bitdefender) to scan. If malware is confirmed and cannot be removed, a factory reset is the definitive solution. iOS is considerably more resistant to malware due to app sandboxing and App Store review, but is not immune to phishing and profile-based attacks.

OS Fails to Update

OS Fails to Update
System update download stalls, installation fails, or update option does not appear
OS / Storage
Insufficient storage
OS updates are large — iOS updates are typically 3–7 GB; Android updates 1–4 GB. The device needs free space approximately equal to 1.5–2× the update size for the download, extraction, and installation process. A device with 2 GB free cannot install a 5 GB update. iOS will show: "Not Enough Space — iOS [version] requires [x] GB of space to update." It may offer to temporarily remove apps to make room. Android will show an error during download or pre-installation check. Resolution: free up storage and retry, or use a computer to update (iTunes/Finder for iOS; Android may update via a PC with vendor-provided update software).
Device no longer supported
The most common reason a device cannot update to a new OS version is that the hardware is no longer supported. Apple typically supports iPhones for 5–6 years — an iPhone 8 cannot run the latest iOS version indefinitely. Android varies by manufacturer — Google Pixel devices receive 5–7 years of OS updates, while budget Android devices may receive only 1–2. When the update option does not appear at all, check the OS vendor's published supported device list. An unsupported device will continue to receive minor security patches for a limited time, then will receive no further updates — representing a security risk that a technician should document and escalate (device replacement is the long-term solution).
MDM-controlled update policy
On MDM-enrolled devices, the organization's IT policy may delay or block OS updates. This is intentional in enterprise environments — IT teams need to test OS updates for compatibility with corporate apps before deploying them to all employees. If a managed device shows "Software Update — Updates are managed by your organization," the update is blocked by MDM policy. The technician should not attempt to override MDM policy — report to the MDM administrator who can schedule or approve the update in the MDM console.
Download failure / network issue
Update downloads fail on unstable networks. Large OS updates should be downloaded on Wi-Fi (most devices block OTA updates on cellular by default for updates over a certain size). Ensure the device is on a stable Wi-Fi connection with a strong signal, plugged into power, and not being actively used during the download. iOS updates can be downloaded in the background overnight with the device on power and Wi-Fi. If the download repeatedly fails, try resetting network settings: iOS: Settings → General → Transfer or Reset iPhone → Reset → Reset Network Settings. This clears saved Wi-Fi passwords and VPN configurations but often resolves stubborn connectivity issues affecting the update server.
iOS update via computer
For iOS devices that cannot update OTA (over the air) due to storage or network issues, updating via a computer bypasses the on-device constraints. Connect the iPhone/iPad to a Mac (Finder → select device) or Windows PC (iTunes). Finder/iTunes downloads the update directly to the computer (where storage and bandwidth are less constrained) and pushes it to the device. This method also allows Recovery Mode updates — if the device is in a boot loop or won't start normally, putting it into Recovery Mode (device-specific button combination) and connecting to iTunes/Finder allows a clean iOS reinstall that preserves or wipes user data depending on the options chosen.

Section DBattery Life Issues

Battery complaints are one of the most frequent mobile support tickets. A technician must distinguish between hardware degradation (the battery's physical capacity has diminished with age and charge cycles) and software drain (an app or setting is consuming power excessively). These require different fixes — the former requires battery replacement, the latter requires settings changes or app removal.

Hardware / Battery Age Hardware
Lithium-ion batteries degrade after 300–500 full charge cycles. Capacity below ~80% causes both reduced battery life and CPU throttling. iOS: Settings → Battery → Battery Health. Android: varies by model. Fix: battery replacement by certified technician.
Rogue App Drain Software
A single app consuming disproportionate CPU/GPS/radio time drains battery abnormally fast. iOS: Settings → Battery → Battery Usage (last 24 hrs / 10 days). Android: Settings → Battery → Battery Usage. Identify and uninstall or restrict the offending app.
Settings-Based Drain Settings
High screen brightness, always-on location services, push email sync every minute, Bluetooth/Wi-Fi scanning in background — all reduce battery life significantly. Reduce brightness, use adaptive/auto-brightness, switch location to "While Using" for most apps, set email to fetch manually or every 30 min.
Environmental / Thermal Environment
Cold temperatures reduce battery capacity temporarily; extreme heat degrades it permanently. A device used in sub-freezing conditions may show rapid drain that recovers at normal temperature. A device left in a hot car (>45°C) sustains permanent capacity loss. Keep devices within -20°C to 45°C operating range.
Battery Life Issues — Detailed Diagnosis
Systematic approach to identifying and resolving abnormal battery drain
Battery / Performance
Check battery usage by app
iOS: Settings → Battery — scroll down to see battery usage by app over the last 24 hours and 10 days. Each app shows percentage of battery consumed plus time on screen vs. background. An app with high background usage that the user doesn't actively use is a drain candidate — disable background app refresh for it (Settings → General → Background App Refresh → toggle off for specific apps). Android: Settings → Battery → Battery Usage — similar per-app breakdown. Sort by usage to identify outliers. An app appearing at the top of the list despite not being actively used indicates abnormal background drain — restrict background activity or uninstall.
Location services
GPS is the single highest power consumer among sensors. Apps set to "Always" for location access (rather than "While Using App") continuously poll the GPS radio in the background. iOS: Settings → Privacy & Security → Location Services — review each app and change from Always to While Using App unless Always is genuinely required (navigation apps while routing). Android: Settings → Location → App permissions — similarly restrict to "Only while using." The "Precise Location" toggle (Android 12+) allows apps to use approximate location instead of GPS, further reducing power consumption.
Screen brightness and timeout
The display is typically the largest single battery consumer. Reduce screen brightness (use adaptive/auto-brightness which adjusts based on ambient light), reduce screen timeout (Auto-Lock on iOS, Screen Timeout on Android) to the shortest acceptable duration (30 seconds to 1 minute). Enable Dark Mode on devices with OLED displays (iPhone X and later, many Android flagships) — OLED pixels that display black are fully off and consume zero power, making Dark Mode genuinely reduce battery drain on OLED screens (no benefit on LCD).
Background App Refresh (iOS)
Background App Refresh allows iOS apps to check for new content while not in use — mail, news, and social apps update so their content is fresh when opened. This consumes battery continuously. Settings → General → Background App Refresh: disable entirely or selectively per app. Disable for apps that don't require up-to-the-minute content (games, utilities, shopping apps). Keep enabled for apps where current content is important (email, messaging). The trade-off: apps with Background App Refresh disabled may show a loading delay when opened as they fetch fresh content.
Push notifications and email sync
Push email (Exchange ActiveSync, Gmail) maintains a persistent connection to the mail server that wakes the device for each new message. Frequent sync intervals increase battery drain. For personal devices, changing email fetch from Push to manual (only syncs when you open the app) or a 30-minute interval significantly extends battery life. iOS: Settings → Mail → Accounts → Fetch New Data — configure per-account. Android: each email app has its own sync frequency setting. On corporate devices, Push is typically required by IT policy for timely communication — do not change without approval.
Wireless radios in background
Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, and cellular radios each consume power. When in an area with no Wi-Fi access points, the device continuously scans for networks — consuming battery with nothing to connect to. Wi-Fi scanning: turn off Wi-Fi when in environments where it won't be used (or use a travel/airplane mode toggle). Bluetooth: disable when no Bluetooth accessories are connected. Cellular: a device in poor signal coverage (1 bar) uses significantly more power than one with strong signal — the radio increases transmit power to maintain connection. This is hardware/environment-driven and not user-configurable; moving to better coverage resolves it.

Section ERandom Reboots

Random Reboots
Device restarts without user action — can occur at idle, during calls, or while using apps
Investigate Cause
Common cause #1
Failing / swollen battery
Common cause #2
Rogue or corrupt app
Common cause #3
OS corruption / pending update
Battery failure
A failing lithium-ion battery is the leading hardware cause of random reboots. When the battery cannot supply sufficient current during peak demand (a phone call, launching an app), the voltage sags below the minimum threshold and the processor shuts down abruptly — which appears as a reboot. This is more common when the battery is at a lower charge level (40–60%) than when fully charged. Confirm: if reboots correlate with higher usage activity or occur at moderate charge levels, check battery health. iOS: Settings → Battery → Battery Health — if below 80%, battery replacement resolves the issue. A swollen battery is an emergency — the device should be powered off and not charged until the battery is replaced, as swollen batteries risk thermal runaway.
Software / app cause
An app that causes a kernel panic or deep system crash triggers an automatic reboot. Diagnose: note whether reboots correlate with using a specific app — if reboots only happen when running App X, that app is the cause. Android Safe Mode disables all third-party apps (only system apps run): hold power button → long-press "Power Off" → tap OK when "Reboot to Safe Mode" appears. The device boots with "Safe Mode" displayed in the corner. If reboots stop in Safe Mode, a third-party app is responsible — uninstall recently installed apps one at a time to identify the culprit. iOS does not have a safe mode equivalent available to end users.
OS update or corruption
A failed or partially applied OS update can cause system instability and random reboots. If reboots started after an update, check whether a new update or patch is available that may address the issue. If the device is stuck in a reboot loop (cannot fully boot before rebooting again), iOS recovery via Finder/iTunes is required. Android recovery varies by manufacturer — most have a Recovery Mode accessible via hardware button combinations (typically Power + Volume Down) that allows a factory reset without needing to fully boot.
Overheating
Devices have thermal protection that triggers shutdown (which may look like a reboot) when internal temperature exceeds safe limits. Common causes: direct sunlight, heavy gaming/video recording for extended periods, charging while running intensive apps, a case that traps heat. The device will display a temperature warning message before shutdown on both iOS and Android. Resolution: remove the case, move to a cooler environment, stop charging while running intensive applications. Persistent overheating under normal use (no heavy tasks, normal environment) indicates a hardware fault — the thermal management system, CPU, or battery may be failing.

Section FConnectivity Issues

Wi-Fi Connectivity Issues

Wi-Fi Connectivity Issues
Cannot connect, connected but no internet, or intermittent drops
Common
Basic triage steps
Standard Wi-Fi troubleshooting sequence for mobile devices: (1) Toggle Wi-Fi off and back on (Settings → Wi-Fi). (2) Forget the network and reconnect (tap the network → Forget, then reconnect and re-enter the password). (3) Restart the device. (4) Restart the router/AP. (5) Reset network settings on the device — this clears all saved Wi-Fi passwords, Bluetooth pairings, and VPN configurations, returning network settings to defaults. iOS: Settings → General → Transfer or Reset iPhone → Reset → Reset Network Settings. Android: Settings → General Management → Reset → Reset Network Settings. After reset, reconnect to Wi-Fi networks by re-entering passwords.
Connected but no internet ("Limited")
A device can show "Connected" to Wi-Fi while having no actual internet access — the device has an IP address and can reach the router, but the router cannot reach the internet. Both iOS and Android indicate this with a warning icon on the Wi-Fi symbol or "No Internet Connection" text. Isolate the issue: check whether other devices on the same Wi-Fi work — if yes, the problem is the specific device (try Forget + reconnect, reset network settings). If no devices work, the problem is the router or ISP — restart the router and modem. Also check whether the device received a valid IP address: iOS: Settings → Wi-Fi → tap network → IP Address field. If it shows 169.254.x.x (APIPA), DHCP failed — the device could not obtain an IP from the router.
Incorrect Wi-Fi password
If a Wi-Fi network's password was recently changed, the device will fail to reconnect with an "Incorrect Password" or "Authentication Error" message. Resolution: forget the network and reconnect with the new password. On Android, authentication errors can also indicate a mismatch between the security type configured on the device and what the AP requires — when manually adding a network, ensure the security type (WPA2, WPA3, WPA2/3 Enterprise) matches the AP configuration.
Enterprise Wi-Fi (802.1X / EAP)
Corporate Wi-Fi networks use 802.1X authentication (WPA2/WPA3 Enterprise) requiring username, password, and often a certificate rather than a simple pre-shared key. Connecting a personal device to enterprise Wi-Fi: select the network → choose the EAP method (PEAP is most common) → enter domain credentials → accept the server certificate if prompted. On MDM-managed devices, the Wi-Fi profile including credentials and certificate is pushed automatically — the user doesn't need to configure anything. If an enterprise Wi-Fi connection fails on a managed device after a password change, the IT admin must update the Wi-Fi profile credentials in the MDM console.
Intermittent drops
Intermittent Wi-Fi that connects but repeatedly drops suggests: weak signal (device is at the edge of coverage — move closer to the AP); channel interference (too many networks on the same channel — use a Wi-Fi analyzer app to check and change the AP's channel); band steering issue (the AP is forcing the device between 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands and the handoff is dropping connections — disable band steering or use separate SSIDs for each band); IP address conflict (another device was assigned the same IP — the DHCP server issued a duplicate; restart the router to clear the lease table).

Bluetooth Connectivity Issues

Bluetooth Connectivity Issues
Cannot pair, paired device won't connect, audio or data transfer problems
Bluetooth / Pairing
Pairing failure
When a Bluetooth device fails to pair, the standard resolution is remove and re-pair: on both the phone and the accessory, remove the existing pairing if any exists (forget the device on both ends). Put the accessory into pairing mode (typically indicated by a flashing LED — refer to the device's manual for the pairing mode procedure). On the phone, go to Settings → Bluetooth → [device name] to pair. Common issues: accessory is not in pairing mode (most common cause — already paired to another device; long-press the pairing button to reset); too far apart (Bluetooth range is approximately 10 meters / 33 feet; obstructions reduce this); Bluetooth radio issue on phone — toggle Bluetooth off/on, or restart the device.
Paired but not connecting
A device that shows as "Paired" in the Bluetooth list but does not connect automatically has lost its connection state. Tap the paired device → Connect (or the (i) icon → Connect). If that fails: forget the device on both the phone and the accessory, re-pair from scratch. Also verify the accessory is not already connected to another device — most Bluetooth accessories maintain only one active connection at a time; connecting to the phone requires disconnecting from the previously connected device.
Audio quality issues
Bluetooth audio problems (choppy audio, static, lag) are typically caused by: interference — 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi and Bluetooth share the same frequency band; crowded Wi-Fi environments degrade Bluetooth audio quality; moving away from the Wi-Fi router or switching Wi-Fi to 5 GHz can help; distance — audio quality degrades as distance and obstructions increase; codec mismatch — if the phone and headphones don't support the same Bluetooth audio codec (SBC, AAC, aptX, LDAC), the connection falls back to lower quality SBC; check both device specs to understand supported codecs; outdated firmware on the Bluetooth accessory — update the accessory's firmware via the manufacturer's app.
Clearing the Bluetooth cache (Android)
Android maintains a Bluetooth cache that can become corrupt, causing all manner of Bluetooth connection failures even after removing and re-pairing devices. Clearing it often resolves persistent Bluetooth problems: Settings → Apps → show system apps → Bluetooth → Storage → Clear Cache. On some Android versions, the Bluetooth service needs to be restarted after clearing — restart the device. This fix has no iOS equivalent (iOS does not expose the Bluetooth stack's cache to users).

NFC (Near-Field Communication) Issues

NFC Issues
NFC payments, tag reading, or peer-to-peer transfer not working
NFC
NFC fundamentals
Near-Field Communication (NFC) is a short-range (≤4 cm / ~1.5 inches) wireless technology used for contactless payments (Apple Pay, Google Pay), reading NFC tags, and peer-to-peer data transfer (Android Beam / Nearby Share). NFC operates at 13.56 MHz and requires the devices to be nearly touching. The very short range is both a limitation (it doesn't work at a distance) and a security feature (accidental reads and eavesdropping are prevented by proximity requirements).
NFC not enabled
On Android, NFC can be manually enabled or disabled. Verify it is on: Settings → Connected Devices → Connection Preferences → NFC → toggle On. On iOS, NFC is always enabled for system-level functions (Apple Pay, App Clips, NFC tag reading) and cannot be manually toggled — it is simply always available. If NFC features appear to not work on iOS, the issue is not the NFC radio but rather the specific application or permission configuration.
Positioning and interference
NFC is extremely position-sensitive. The NFC antenna location varies by device model — it is typically near the top of the phone on iPhone, and near the center or bottom on many Android phones. The user must place the device correctly for NFC to register. Metal cases block NFC entirely — a metal phone case prevents all NFC functions including tap-to-pay. Thick or wallet cases can impede the signal. Solution: remove the case, or at minimum hold the phone in the correct orientation for the antenna location. Additionally, phone cards or credit cards stored in a wallet case behind the phone can cause NFC interference when making payments.
Contactless payment failures
For Apple Pay or Google Pay NFC payment failures: (1) Verify NFC is enabled (Android) or the device supports NFC payments (iPhone 6 and later). (2) Verify a card is set up in the wallet app (Wallet on iOS, Google Wallet on Android). (3) Verify authentication — Apple Pay requires Face ID, Touch ID, or passcode; Google Pay requires screen unlock. (4) Ensure the payment terminal supports the payment method — older terminals may not support NFC. (5) Try removing and re-adding the card in the wallet app. (6) Check whether a corporate MDM policy has disabled payment apps — some enterprise MDM profiles restrict mobile payment functionality.

Section GScreen Does Not Autorotate

Screen Does Not Autorotate
Display stays in portrait when device is rotated — or rotates when it should not
Accelerometer / Settings
Most common cause
Rotation lock enabled
Hardware sensor
Accelerometer / gyroscope
App override
Some apps lock to one orientation
Rotation lock — first check
The single most common cause of a screen that will not rotate is Portrait Orientation Lock (iOS) or Auto-rotate being disabled (Android). This is the correct first thing to check — it is often accidentally enabled by a swipe in the Control Center. iOS: swipe down from the top-right corner (iPhone X+) or up from the bottom (older iPhones) to open Control Center → look for the lock icon with a circle arrow → if highlighted/red, tap it to disable the lock. Android: swipe down from the top to open Quick Settings → look for "Auto-rotate" or "Portrait" toggle → enable Auto-rotate. On some Android devices, this setting also appears at: Settings → Display → Auto-rotate screen.
App-level orientation lock
Some apps are designed to display only in one orientation regardless of the device's rotation lock state or sensor readings. For example, many games, video players (while playing), and some banking apps force landscape or portrait mode by design. This is not a device fault — it is intentional app behavior. If rotation works in the home screen and other apps but not in a specific app, the app itself is locking the orientation. There is no user-accessible fix for this; the app developer controls orientation support. Verify by testing rotation in multiple apps — if most apps rotate correctly, the device's rotation hardware and settings are fine.
Accelerometer fault
Screen rotation is driven by the device's accelerometer (which detects gravitational direction) and sometimes the gyroscope (which detects angular velocity). A faulty or miscalibrated accelerometer causes the screen to not respond to physical rotation — or to rotate incorrectly (showing landscape when portrait, or constantly shifting). Test: after confirming rotation lock is off, lay the phone flat, then rotate it slowly — if autorotation works for some orientations but not others, or responds erratically, the accelerometer may be faulty. On Android, some diagnostic apps (such as manufacturer diagnostic tools) can run sensor tests to verify accelerometer function. A hardware accelerometer fault requires device repair.
Software glitch — restart
A software glitch in the rotation service can prevent rotation even with the lock disabled and the accelerometer functioning correctly. A simple device restart typically resolves this — it resets the sensor services and rotation handler. If rotation fails after restart, proceed to the accelerometer fault investigation above. On Android, clearing the cache partition (via Recovery Mode) can resolve OS-level sensor service issues without a factory reset. On iOS, there is no equivalent — if restart doesn't fix it, the next step is checking for an iOS update or restoring the device.
iPad — platform behavior
iPads support a Side Switch or software Rotation Lock accessible from the Control Center, identical in function to the iPhone's Portrait Orientation Lock. On older iPads with a physical Side Switch, the switch can be configured (Settings → General → Use Side Switch to → Lock Rotation) to toggle rotation lock physically — pressing the physical switch locks rotation. Users who don't realize their Side Switch is configured this way may be confused when the iPad won't rotate. Check Settings → General to see what the Side Switch is mapped to.

Mobile OS and Application Troubleshooting — Scenario Responses
App opens, immediately closes back to home screen
→
(1) Force-close app → restart device. (2) Check storage — if under 10% free, free space. (3) Check app permissions (Settings → Apps/Privacy). (4) Clear app cache (Android) or delete + reinstall (iOS). (5) Check for OS/app update compatibility issues.
App freezes and won't close
→
Force-close via app switcher (swipe away). If touch screen is unresponsive: force restart — iPhone: Volume Up → Volume Down → hold Side button. Android: hold power 10–15 seconds. After reboot, if app keeps freezing: clear cache/data or reinstall.
App update stuck "Waiting" in the store
→
Check network connectivity. Check available storage. Android: Settings → Apps → Play Store → Clear Cache → retry. iOS: Sign out of App Store (Settings → [name] → Sign Out) → sign back in → retry. Also try: tap the waiting update in the store to pause, then tap again to resume.
Corporate app shows "Untrusted Developer" on iOS
→
Settings → General → VPN & Device Management → [enterprise developer name] → Trust. This must be done after every device restore. If the developer certificate has expired, the IT admin must renew it and redistribute the app — the technician cannot fix this locally.
Device is very slow — user says "it's been slow for months"
→
Check battery health first (iOS: Settings → Battery → Battery Health; Android: varies). If below 80%, battery replacement resolves CPU throttling. Check storage (free up if under 15% free). Check battery usage for rogue apps. Restart device. If still slow: factory reset as last resort.
iOS update says "Not Enough Space"
→
iOS will offer to temporarily remove some apps to make room — accept this offer (apps re-download after update). Or: manually free storage (Settings → General → iPhone Storage → identify large items). Last option: update via Mac/Finder or PC/iTunes — bypasses on-device storage constraint.
Android device randomly reboots — happens during calls
→
Reboots during high-demand moments (calls) strongly suggest battery failure. Check battery health app or have it tested. If battery is swollen, power off immediately and arrange battery replacement. Also boot into Safe Mode (hold power → long-press Power Off) — if reboots stop in Safe Mode, a third-party app is responsible.
Wi-Fi says "Connected" but no internet on one device
→
Other devices work on same Wi-Fi → problem is the specific device. Forget the network and reconnect. Check IP address — if 169.254.x.x (APIPA), DHCP failed; restart router. Reset Network Settings if reconnect doesn't work. Try toggling Airplane Mode on/off to force radio re-initialization.
Bluetooth headphones paired but audio still comes from phone speaker
→
Device is paired but audio output not routed to headphones. Manually tap the device in Bluetooth settings → Connect. Check media volume separately from ringtone volume (some devices have independent controls). If the headphones connected but audio routing failed: forget and re-pair. Check for a firmware update for the headphones.
NFC tap-to-pay fails at checkout
→
Check: (1) NFC enabled (Android Settings → Connected Devices → NFC). (2) Card set up in Wallet/Google Wallet. (3) Authentication completed (biometric/passcode). (4) Metal case removed. (5) Correct antenna placement for device model. (6) Terminal supports contactless — if terminal shows no NFC symbol, it may not support it. Try re-adding the card in the wallet app.
Screen won't rotate on iPhone — rotation lock is off
→
Rotation lock is confirmed off → test in multiple apps. If it works in some apps but not others, those apps lock orientation by design (app issue, not device). If rotation fails in ALL apps including home screen: restart the device. If still broken after restart: accelerometer hardware fault — check for iOS update, then consider device service/repair.

High-Probability Exam Scenarios

Scenario 1: An Android user's app randomly crashes throughout the day. The technician notices the app hasn't been updated in six months. What is the most appropriate first step? → Check the Play Store for a pending app update and install it. A developer-released fix is the most targeted solution for a crashing app before resorting to clearing data or reinstalling.

Scenario 2: A user's iPhone has been getting progressively slower over the past year. They report that it was fast when new. What is the most likely cause and solution? → Battery degradation causing CPU throttling. Check Settings → Battery → Battery Health & Charging. If below ~80%, battery replacement will restore normal performance. This is a hardware issue, not a software problem.

Scenario 3: An Android device keeps randomly rebooting. A technician boots it into Safe Mode and the reboots stop. What does this indicate? → A third-party app is causing the reboots. In Safe Mode all third-party apps are disabled; stability in Safe Mode confirms the OS and hardware are not at fault. Resolution: uninstall recently installed apps one at a time (most recently installed first) to identify the culprit.

Scenario 4: A user reports their iPhone won't rotate to landscape. The screen rotation lock is confirmed OFF in Control Center. The app switcher is tested and home screen also won't rotate. What is the next step? → Restart the device. A software glitch in the rotation service is the likely cause. If restart doesn't fix it, investigate accelerometer hardware fault and check for an iOS update.

Scenario 5: A corporate iPhone user downloaded a company app from an internal distribution link. The app icon has an "X" on it and it won't open, displaying "Untrusted Enterprise Developer." What must the technician do? → Settings → General → VPN & Device Management → find the enterprise developer → tap Trust. This explicitly grants trust to the developer's enterprise certificate, allowing the app to run.

Scenario 6: A user's Android device Wi-Fi shows Connected to the corporate network but cannot browse any websites. Other users on the same network work fine. The device's IP shows 169.254.3.42. What is the diagnosis? → The device has an APIPA address (169.254.x.x), meaning DHCP failed — it could not obtain a valid IP from the router. Fix: Forget the Wi-Fi network and reconnect, or restart the device and router to trigger a fresh DHCP lease. If persistent, Reset Network Settings.

Scenario 7: A user reports they cannot install a Google Play app on their company Android device, receiving the message "Installation blocked by administrator." What is the correct response? → This is an MDM policy restriction. The technician should escalate to the MDM administrator to either approve the app and push it through the managed app catalog, or grant the user an exception. This cannot be resolved by the user or tier-1 technician.
